What happens when people stop using AI?

We conducted an experiment with 625 everyday ChatGPT users to find out what happens when people stop using AI. What we found is that when people stop using AI, they report feeling less lonely and engage in more social activities.

How did we run the experiment?

We recruited 625 people in the U.S. who already used ChatGPT at least 15 minutes a day and had accounts on the platform for at least one month.

Half were assigned to stop using AI chatbots for two weeks (N=306). The other half stopped for just one day (N=319). We used a browser extension to block access to 30+ popular AI assistants, including ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Our browser extension also monitored compliance with the experiment.

Everyone first completed a baseline survey measuring their well-being, loneliness, and social activity. Participants were then randomly assigned to stop using AI chatbots for either one day or two weeks, and completed a midpoint survey one week in. At the end of the two weeks, everyone took an endline survey and was free to use AI again. Two weeks later, we followed up with everyone to see whether the changes lasted.

Who were these participants?

Participants were recruited using Prolific, an online platform for recruiting research participants. Most participants used AI assistants for seeking out information or for getting how-to advice. Conversations about relationships or personal reflection made up just 3.2% of their conversations with ChatGPT.

What were the other critera for participants to be eligible for the study?

To be eligible for the study, participants had to be at least 18 years old, based in the U.S, and use ChatGPT as their primary AI assistant. They also had to self-report as using ChatGPT for at least 15 minutes a day, have an account on the platform for at least one month, be willing to accept a payment of $40/week to stop using AI, and use Chrome as their browser.

Participants felt less lonely and were more social after they stopped using AI

Compared with participants who took a one-day break, those who stopped using AI for two weeks experienced a greater reduction in loneliness. By the end of the break, 58.2% of the two-week group felt less lonely than when they started, 22.1% felt no change, and 19.8% felt more lonely.

How do we measure loneliness?

We asked participants three questions about how often they felt isolated, left out, or lacking companionship. Each question was rated on a 7-point scale, and the responses were added together to produce a loneliness score ranging from 0 to 18. Higher scores indicate greater loneliness.

How big is the change in loneliness?

The difference was modest. On a loneliness questionnaire scored from 0 to 18, participants taking a two-week break experienced a reduction of about half a point more than those taking a one-day break.

Did people change the time spent working when they stopped using AI?

No. We found no significant difference between the groups in how much time people spent working each day.

What might explain these changes?

We asked whether changes in participants' social behaviors one week into the study predicted the decline inloneliness at the end of it. We looked at both the quantity of social interaction (time spent socializing) and the quality of these social interaction (satisfaction with social interactions).

Both quantity and quality of social interaction seemed to play a part. A week into the break, participants were spending more time socializing and felt somewhat more satisfied with their social interactions than those who had only stopped using AI for one day. Participants who saw these changes also tended to feel less lonely by the end of the two weeks.

Reflecting on changes in their social behavior

Participants in the two-week group reflected on how they thought their social life had changed during the study period. About 41.5% described their social interactions improving, 42.3% noticed little or no change, and 8.8% said things got worse or were mixed. Grouping the responses by topic, a few clusters stood out. Who benefited the most from taking a break from AI?

Contrary to expectations, we found that how much time someone spent using ChatGPT per day made no difference in their change in loneliness.

However, what did matter was how long participants had been using ChatGPT for. People who had used ChatGPT for longer saw bigger drops in loneliness. One interpretation of this result is that AI usage is more ingrained in these participants' everyday routines, making the changes more pronounced.

Two weeks after resuming AI usage, differences in loneliness remained

Once the study period ended, all participants were free to resume using AI.

Two weeks after the experiment ended, participants who took the two-week break still reported feeling less lonely compared to those in the one-day group, scoring about 0.75 points lower on our 0-18-point loneliness scale.

They also reported feeling more competent (capable of handling things) and more autonomous (in charge of their own choices). Participants in the two-week group also reported that they were using AI less frequently and less intensively compared to those in the one-day group.
